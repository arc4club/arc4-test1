Jumpstart Trading➔

Trading is entirely about how you manage your risk and psychology — no amount of analysis or technique will help you in the long run. Risk and psychology are deeply correlated: the more emotional you are, the poorer the decisions you're bound to make.

Before the Trade

Say to yourself

  • I don't know what will happen next.
  • The outcome of the trade is not tied to my self-worth.
  • Just follow it. Timing a pullback is still trying to reverse. Follow.
  • This trade is not important.
  • I don't have to give a reason to myself — or to a viewer — for why I'm taking a trade.
  • Don't get in when it has already made the move; you gotta do it before. It's okay to guess, it's okay to not know what you're doing — just do it.

Do not

  • Try to reverse, including timing pullbacks at their farthest point.
  • Get in on a big move — most of them don't continue.
  • Overanalyze.

Remember

  • Every trade exists independently.
  • It's just a bunch of rectangles. Chill out.
  • Old fears about trading resembling a past gambling problem don't have to repeat — that's a choice, not a certainty.
During the Trade
  • It's okay to see a big profit turn into a little profit — that's how you eventually get the big wins too.
  • Be cautious chasing profit near a high-of-day or low-of-day — big reversals often happen fast right at, barely below, or barely above those levels.
  • If you can't handle the pressure of watching a trade, don't watch it. Do something else.
  • Don't chase your stop loss.
After the Trade

Classify every trade into one of three types:

Good trade that didn't work

  • That's all you can do. Process over profits. Don't feel bad for yourself — it's part of the game.

Good trade that worked

  • Don't falsely attribute the reasons it worked. You can't always know for certain why a trade worked — stay level-headed and remove the emotional association from the money.

Bad — didn't follow the rules to enter

  • Update your trading notes to prevent it from happening again. Process over profits.
Survival Rules
  • Survive the curve: practice, study, size down your capital, take fewer trades, and focus on surviving the initial learning phase.
  • Accept losses: expect them and handle them like an adult. There's no strategy that avoids every red day — the trick is not blowing up your account when it happens.
  • Execute the edge: develop a clear edge, believe in it, and execute it mechanically every time you see it. Set strict risk parameters and let the trade play out to your stop loss or take-profit.
  • Control the vices: don't be impatient or greedy. Never trade out of boredom, and don't force your ideas onto a chart hoping the market agrees with you.
  • Be realistic: you don't need to understand every candle or catch every move. Take what the market gives you, protect your capital, and try again the next day.
"Keep your head high, King. The money doesn't matter as long as you lose less than your monthly limit. Take lots of trades, because you don't really learn anything from just watching — your brain filters out the 'pretend' trades and skews them in your favor. Just press the buy button."
⚠ The 90/10 split

Psychology and risk management make up 90% of trading. The remaining 10% is the technical side — knowing what buttons to click, identifying trends, and basic mechanics.

The Reality of the Learning Curve
  • The technicals: roughly 2–3 hours of daily study to learn the technical side of charts.
  • The psychology: controlling your emotions takes years. You can never fully eliminate emotional responses — the goal is reducing the negative effects of bad decisions.
  • The progress: you might revenge trade daily at first. After 2–3 years of discipline, the urge still shows up, but you might only give in once every 2–3 months. Progress is about frequency and control, not perfection.
The Evolutionary Mismatch

Your brain evolved for survival, not for generating trading profits. Every instinct that kept your ancestors alive in the wild will actively work against your account. Before your trading day, run through these diagnostic questions:

  • Do you increase your position size immediately after a win?
  • Do you hold onto losing trades longer than you hold your winners?
  • Do you make impulsive revenge trades right after a loss?
  • Do you check your open positions obsessively every few minutes?
  • Do you blame your losses on market "manipulation" instead of your own execution?
Example: Real Trade Reflection
"You are successfully identifying the thought processes that made you trade so poorly the first day. When it's green with momentum you want to get in, but that's when it reverses. It's a balance between trading the opposite of what you think, and getting into a trade at the right time."
